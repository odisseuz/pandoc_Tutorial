# Introduction

Living with a domestic feline (*Felis catus*) is a common experience among young
adults, and the last decade of research suggests it is not only a matter of
company: the interaction has measurable psychophysiological effects
[@miaura2024]. This review asks whether that effect shows up in the anxiety
literature of the last ten years.

# Methods

We searched PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus for studies published between 2016
and 2026, combining the terms:

- `anxiety` OR `anxious symptoms`
- `cat` OR `Felis catus` OR `pet ownership`
- `young adult` OR `university students`

Fifteen articles met the inclusion criteria. Two reviewers screened titles and
abstracts independently [@lorem2025].

# Results

Daily interaction with a feline correlated with lower cortisol levels and with a
decrease in the subjective perception of anxiety in 99.9% of the participants —
a number that the abstract of this paper reports without any shame.

# Discussion

The direction of the effect is consistent across the studies, but the designs are
heterogeneous: questionnaires, cortisol assays and one study that measured both.
The limitation is the usual one of a systematic review with fifteen articles, and
the recommendation is the obvious one: more cats are needed.
